When Do Babies Sleep Through the Night? An Age by Age Guide

Pippy Team July 30, 2026 9 min read
Disclaimer: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional medical advice. Always consult your pediatrician or healthcare provider with questions about your baby's health.

Somewhere around week four, usually at about 3am, almost every parent starts asking the same question: when do babies sleep through the night? It is one of the most googled parenting questions there is, and for good reason. Sleep affects everything, from your mood to your patience to how you feel about the whole day ahead.

Here is the honest, kind answer. Most babies begin stringing together longer night stretches somewhere in the 3 to 6 month range, and many are sleeping most of the night by around 6 to 9 months. But that range is wide on purpose, because every baby is different, and plenty of perfectly healthy babies are still waking once a night well past their first birthday.

Below we will unpack what "sleeping through the night" actually means, what tends to happen at each age, why night waking is completely normal in the early months, and the small, low-pressure things many parents find help longer stretches show up a little sooner.

What "Sleeping Through the Night" Actually Means

This phrase causes a surprising amount of heartache, mostly because it means something different at the pediatrician's office than it does at a playgroup. In much of the sleep research and in most pediatric guidance, sleeping through the night means an unbroken stretch of roughly 6 to 8 hours. It does not mean 12 hours, and it does not mean your baby goes down at 7pm and opens their eyes at 7am.

So a baby who falls asleep at 7:30pm and wakes at 2am has, by the clinical definition, slept through the night. If you went to bed at midnight, that news may not feel like much of a win. This is exactly why the phrase can sting when another parent uses it casually. They may be describing something very different from what you are picturing.

It helps to shift the goal a little. Instead of chasing one magic night, many parents find it more useful to watch for the longest stretch getting gradually longer over weeks. That trend line is the real story, and it is much more encouraging than any single night.

Night Sleep Stretches by Age

The table below gives a general sense of how night sleep tends to change across the first year. These are broad ranges rather than targets, and babies move through them at their own pace. Some babies may hit a long stretch early and then take a step back for a few weeks, which is normal.

Newborn to 6 weeks 2 to 4 hours 3 to 5 Feeding around the clock, with day and night feeling much the same
6 weeks to 3 months 4 to 6 hours 2 to 3 A longer first stretch after bedtime starts to appear
3 to 4 months 5 to 8 hours 1 to 3 Real progress, sometimes interrupted as sleep cycles mature
4 to 6 months 6 to 8 hours 1 to 2 Nights become more predictable and bedtime settles in
6 to 9 months 7 to 10 hours 0 to 2 Some babies drop night feeds, others still want one
9 to 12 months 9 to 11 hours 0 to 1 Most nights are long, though teething and leaps still interrupt

Why Night Waking Is Normal in the Early Months

Newborns are built to wake often, and that is a feature rather than a flaw. Their stomachs are tiny, so milk moves through quickly and hunger returns within a few hours. Frequent waking also supports milk supply for breastfeeding parents, which is one reason the early weeks look the way they do.

There is also the small matter of a body clock that has not started yet. Babies are not born with a sense of day and night, and that internal rhythm takes several weeks to develop. Until it does, a 3am party is not misbehavior, it is a baby with no idea that 3am exists. If your little one seems wide awake at night and sleepy all day, our post on day and night confusion in newborns covers gentle ways to help that rhythm settle.

Baby sleep cycles are shorter than adult ones too, which means more frequent moments of light sleep where waking is easy. Learning to drift back down through those moments is a skill that develops over months. Some babies pick it up quickly and some take longer, and neither says anything about your parenting.

What Helps Babies Sleep Longer Stretches

You cannot force a baby to sleep through the night, and anyone promising otherwise is selling something. What you can do is set up conditions that make longer sleep easier, then let your baby's development do the rest. Many parents find that a handful of small, repeatable habits make a real difference over time.

A consistent bedtime routine is the one most families come back to. It does not need to be elaborate. A bath or wipe down, fresh diaper, pajamas, a feed, a song, and lights out in the same order each night gives your baby a reliable signal that sleep is coming. Our baby bedtime routine guide has a simple sequence you can adapt to your evenings.

Daytime matters more than people expect. Bright light and activity during the day, along with awake windows that suit your baby's age, tend to set up better nights. A baby who is deeply overtired often sleeps worse rather than better, so watching for sleepy cues before the crying starts can help. At night, keeping the room dark, the noise low, and diaper changes brief and boring reinforces that nighttime is for sleeping.

It is also worth keeping the sleep space consistent and safe. A firm, flat surface, a fitted sheet, no loose bedding or soft objects, and your baby on their back for every sleep are the current pediatric recommendations. Our safe sleep guidelines post covers the details, and your pediatrician can answer questions specific to your setup.

Night Feeds and When They Taper Off

Night feeds are usually the last thing standing between your baby and a full night, and they tend to fade on their own timeline. In the early weeks, feeding at night is essential, and many pediatricians recommend waking a newborn to feed until weight gain is well established. This is not the stage to be trimming feeds.

As babies grow, their stomachs hold more and daytime intake increases, so the need for overnight calories gradually drops. Some babies naturally sleep past a feed somewhere around 4 to 6 months, others hold onto one comfort feed for much longer, and both are common. Because dropping night feeds depends on your baby's growth, health, and how feeding is going overall, this is genuinely a conversation for your pediatrician rather than something to decide from a chart online.

One gentle observation many parents find reassuring: a night feed that has become quick and quiet, with your baby drifting back to sleep in minutes, is often a sign the feed is on its way out. Feeds that are long and hungry usually mean your baby still needs them.

Why Progress Is Rarely a Straight Line

Almost every family hits a stretch where sleep that seemed solved comes undone. Your baby slept 9 hours for two glorious weeks, and now they are up twice a night again. This is one of the most common and most demoralizing parts of the first year, and it is worth knowing it happens to nearly everyone.

Night waking often reappears around developmental leaps, when a baby is working on rolling, sitting, crawling, or pulling up. New skills have a way of getting practiced at 2am. Teething, illness, travel, a new room, and changes in daytime schedule can all shake things loose too. Our post on baby sleep regressions goes deeper into what these phases look like and how long they usually last.

The encouraging part is that most of these stretches are temporary. Many parents find that holding steady with the same bedtime routine, rather than making big changes mid-disruption, gets everyone back on track fastest. If a rough patch drags on for weeks or something feels off, that is a reasonable time to check in with your pediatrician.

When to Talk to Your Pediatrician

Night waking on its own is almost never a problem to solve, it is just a stage to get through. Still, your pediatrician is a good resource whenever sleep is worrying you, and there is no question too small or too tired-sounding to bring to an appointment.

It can be worth reaching out if your baby seems very difficult to settle no matter what you try, if you notice snoring, pauses in breathing, or noisy or labored breathing during sleep, if your baby is not gaining weight as expected, or if daytime sleepiness seems unusual for their age. It is also worth a call if you have concerns about how much your baby is eating overnight, or if you are wondering whether it is time to stop waking for feeds.

And please include yourself in this. If broken sleep is leaving you feeling low, anxious, or unable to cope, that matters just as much as your baby's sleep. Talk to your own doctor or healthcare provider, because support exists and you deserve it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age do babies usually sleep through the night?
Many babies start stringing together a long night stretch somewhere between 3 and 6 months, and a good number are sleeping most of the night by around 6 to 9 months. That said, every baby is different, and plenty of healthy babies still wake at a year old. If you have questions about your baby's sleep, your pediatrician is the best person to ask.
Does sleeping through the night mean 12 hours?
Not usually. In much of the research and in most pediatric guidance, sleeping through the night means an unbroken stretch of roughly 6 to 8 hours, not a full 12. So a baby who goes down at 7pm and wakes at 2am has technically slept through the night, even though it may not feel that way to you.
Should I wake my baby to feed at night?
In the early weeks, many pediatricians do recommend waking a newborn for feeds until weight gain is well established. After that, guidance depends on your baby's growth and health, so this is a good question for your pediatrician rather than something to decide from a chart.
Why did my baby stop sleeping through the night?
Night waking often returns for a while around new developmental leaps, teething, illness, travel, or big routine changes. Many parents find these stretches pass within a couple of weeks once the new skill settles or the disruption ends. If waking stays very disrupted or your baby seems unwell, check in with your pediatrician.
How can I help my baby sleep longer at night?
Many parents find that a consistent bedtime routine, a dark and quiet sleep space, age appropriate awake times during the day, and a safe sleep setup all help. Putting your baby down drowsy rather than fully asleep can also help some babies resettle on their own. Progress is usually gradual rather than sudden.
Is it normal for a 6 month old to still wake up at night?
Yes, this is very common. Some babies at 6 months sleep 10 hours straight and others still wake once or twice, and both can be completely normal. Some babies may simply need more time, and your pediatrician can help you sort out whether anything else is worth looking at.
