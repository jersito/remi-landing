When Do Babies Pull Up to Stand? A Milestone Guide

Pippy Team August 4, 2026 9 min read
Disclaimer: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional medical advice. Always consult your pediatrician or healthcare provider with questions about your baby's health.

One day your baby is sitting happily on the rug, and the next you turn around to find them gripping the edge of the coffee table, knees shaking, face lit up like they have just discovered gravity has a workaround. It is one of those milestones that arrives with almost no warning, and it changes the shape of your whole house.

So when do babies pull up to stand? Most babies get there somewhere between 8 and 11 months, though plenty start earlier and plenty take a bit longer. It usually follows sitting steadily and pushing up onto hands and knees, and it opens the door to cruising and, eventually, those first wobbly independent steps.

Below we will walk through the typical timing, how the skill builds piece by piece, what tends to come next, and the practical things many parents find worth doing once a baby starts hauling themselves upright on the furniture.

When Babies Usually Pull Up to Stand

The commonly cited window for pulling up to stand is roughly 8 to 11 months, with many babies landing around 9 months. Some babies surprise everyone by hauling themselves up at 6 or 7 months, usually strong sitters who have found a conveniently placed couch. Others are still perfectly content on the floor at 11 months and then go from nothing to standing in the space of a week.

What matters more than the exact month is the direction of travel. Babies build gross motor skills in a rough sequence, and each new skill borrows strength and balance from the one before it. A baby who is sitting confidently, rotating to reach toys, and getting up onto hands and knees is building exactly the trunk and hip strength that pulling up requires, even if standing has not happened yet.

It is also worth mentioning that babies born prematurely are usually assessed on adjusted age rather than birth age, which can shift these ranges by a couple of months. Every baby is different, and if your baby's timing feels off to you, your pediatrician can look at the whole picture rather than a single milestone in isolation.

How the Skill Builds, Step by Step

Pulling up looks like one action, but it is really three stacked together. First a baby has to get a grip on something stable. Then they pull their upper body toward it and shift onto their knees. Finally they bring one foot forward, plant it, and push up through that leg into standing. That last piece is the hardest, and it is why so many babies spend a week or two stuck happily in a half kneeling position.

The strength for all of this comes from earlier work. Time on the floor, rolling, reaching across the body, and sitting unsupported all build the core and hip stability that standing depends on. If your baby is still working on the earlier pieces, our guides on when babies sit up and when babies start crawling cover what tends to come first and how to support it gently.

Something many parents find surprising is that not every baby follows the sequence neatly. Some skip crawling entirely and go from sitting to pulling up. Some pull up before they can get themselves back down, which makes for a memorable few weeks. Some cruise for months before letting go. The order shuffles more often than the charts suggest, and a shuffled order on its own is rarely a concern.

Signs Your Baby Is Getting Close

The clearest early hint is weight bearing. If you hold your baby upright with their feet on your lap or the floor and they push down and straighten their legs rather than folding, that is the leg strength piece coming online. Many babies love this game long before they can do it on their own, and doing it for a minute at a time is good practice.

Another common tell is reaching upward. A baby who starts grabbing at the edge of the couch, your knee, or the side of a laundry basket while sitting nearby is testing whether things hold their weight. Around the same time you may notice them getting into a half kneel, one knee down and one foot planted, which is the exact position that pulling up requires.

Frustration is a sign too, oddly enough. A baby who suddenly gets cranky on the floor near furniture is often working on something just out of reach of their current abilities. Many parents find that a burst of fussiness or lighter sleep shows up right before a big motor leap and settles again once the new skill clicks.

What Comes After Pulling Up

Cruising is usually next. Once your baby can stand while holding on, they start taking sideways steps along the couch, the coffee table, or the front of the dishwasher. At first they will keep both hands on the furniture and shuffle. Over time they get bolder, drop to one hand, and eventually make short reaches between two pieces of furniture, which is essentially walking with a safety net.

Somewhere in there most babies learn to lower themselves back down, which is a genuine relief for everyone. Squatting down under control takes more coordination than standing up does, so it often lags by a few weeks. Until then, expect to be summoned to rescue a stranded baby fairly often, including in the middle of the night.

After cruising comes standing without support, then those first steps. The full range for walking is wide, roughly 9 to 15 months, and a baby who cruises confidently at 10 months might still not walk until 14 months. Making Pulling Up Safe at Home

Once a baby can pull up, your home essentially becomes a new environment. The safe zone that used to be floor level now extends to about three feet up, and everything within that band is suddenly reachable. Table edges, cords, tablecloths, and anything perched on a low shelf all come into play at once.

The single highest value fix is anchoring furniture. Dressers, bookcases, and TVs can tip when a baby uses them to pull up, and this is one of the genuinely serious hazards of this stage. Wall anchors are inexpensive and widely available. Beyond that, many parents find it helps to clear low surfaces, tuck away cords, and add soft corner covers to the coffee table that has now become the primary climbing apparatus. Our baby proofing checklist walks through this room by room if you want a fuller sweep.

Falls are going to happen, and most of them are small and part of learning. A soft rug or play mat in the area your baby practices most takes the sting out of the majority of them. Barefoot is generally best indoors for balance and grip. And while it is tempting to hover, giving your baby room to wobble, sit down hard, and try again is how the skill actually develops. Being nearby without steadying every attempt is the sweet spot.

Pulling Up in the Crib at Night

This one catches nearly everyone off guard. A baby who has just learned to pull up will often practice at 2am, standing at the crib rail, delighted and completely unable to get back down. Sleep gets bumpy for a stretch, and it is one of the more common reasons parents suddenly find themselves back to multiple wakeups after weeks of things going well.

The practical first step is lowering the crib mattress, ideally before your baby is pulling up rather than after. General guidance is to drop it once your baby can push up onto hands and knees, and to move it to the lowest setting once standing begins, so the rail stays high enough to prevent tipping or climbing over. If you are still sorting out sleep space logistics, our post on moving from bassinet to crib covers the transition.

For the standing itself, practice during the day is usually more effective than intervention at night. Helping your baby lower from standing to sitting a few times a day, guiding their hands down and bending their knees, teaches the skill much faster than doing it for them at 3am. When it does happen overnight, many parents find that laying the baby back down calmly, with as little talking and light as possible, and repeating it without fuss works better than a long soothing routine. This phase usually passes within a couple of weeks once the sitting down skill catches up.

When to Check In With Your Pediatrician

Milestone ranges are wide on purpose, and most babies who are a little behind one marker are simply taking their own route. That said, there are a few patterns worth mentioning at a checkup rather than waiting out. Not bearing weight on the legs when held upright by around 12 months, or not attempting to pull up at all by around 12 months, is worth a conversation.

It is also worth raising if your baby consistently uses one side of the body much more than the other, if their legs feel unusually stiff or unusually floppy when you handle them, or if they lose a skill they previously had. Losing a skill in particular is always worth a call rather than a wait and see, even though the explanation is often something ordinary like an illness or a growth spurt.

And if you simply have a nagging feeling that something is different about how your baby moves, that is reason enough. Pediatricians would far rather look at a baby who turns out to be perfectly on track than miss a chance to help early. Frequently Asked Questions

When do babies pull up to stand?
Most babies pull up to stand somewhere between 8 and 11 months, though some start as early as 6 or 7 months and others take until closer to a year. It usually shows up after sitting steadily and getting up onto hands and knees. Every baby is different, and your pediatrician is the right person to ask if you are unsure about your baby's timing.
Is it normal for a baby to pull up but not crawl?
Yes, this is fairly common. Some babies skip traditional crawling entirely and go from scooting or rolling straight to pulling up and cruising. Crawling is not a required step on the way to walking. If your baby is moving their body around in some consistent way and using both sides fairly evenly, that is usually reassuring, but it is always fine to raise it at a checkup.
What comes after pulling up to stand?
Cruising usually comes next, which is when a baby holds onto furniture and takes sideways steps along it. After cruising, babies often practice standing without support for a few seconds at a time, then let go and take their first independent steps. Many babies walk somewhere between 9 and 15 months, and that whole range is considered typical.
Why does my baby pull up in the crib and cry?
Many babies learn to pull up before they learn to sit back down, so they end up standing at the crib rail with no idea how to get out of it. This phase often disrupts sleep for a week or two. Many parents find it helps to practice lowering down to sitting during the day, and to keep nighttime help calm and brief so the practice does not become a game.
Should I lower the crib mattress when my baby pulls up?
Yes, and ideally a little before. The general guidance is to lower the crib mattress once your baby can push up on hands and knees, since pulling up often follows quickly. Lowering it to the lowest setting keeps the rail high enough to prevent climbing or tipping over the side.
When should I talk to my pediatrician about pulling up?
It is worth checking in if your baby is not bearing weight on their legs by around 12 months, is not attempting to pull up by around 12 months, uses only one side of the body consistently, or seems to lose a skill they previously had. None of these automatically mean something is wrong, but they are all good reasons to ask your pediatrician rather than wait.
