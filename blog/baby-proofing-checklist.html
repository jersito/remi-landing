Baby Proofing Checklist: When to Start, Room by Room

By the Pippy Care Team August 2, 2026 9 min read
Disclaimer: This article is for informational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional medical advice. Always consult your pediatrician or healthcare provider with questions about your baby's health.

There is a specific moment most parents remember. You set your baby down on the rug, turn around to grab a glass of water, and when you look back they are three feet from where you left them, reaching for a lamp cord with real determination. Nothing bad happens. But something shifts, and suddenly your living room looks completely different to you.

That is usually when the search for a baby proofing checklist begins, and it can feel overwhelming fast. Search around and you will find lists with two hundred items, half of which involve buying something. The good news is that a small number of changes handle most of the serious risk, and the rest can be done gradually as your baby's skills change.

This guide walks through when many parents start, which fixes matter most, and what to look at in each room. Every home is different and every baby is different, so treat this as a starting point rather than a rulebook. Your pediatrician is a great person to ask about anything specific to your baby or your living situation.

When to Start Baby Proofing

Many parents find that somewhere around four to six months is a comfortable time to begin. That may sound early when your baby is still mostly staying where you put them, but mobility tends to arrive suddenly rather than gradually. A baby who has been rolling in one direction for weeks can be scooting across a room within days, and the middle of that transition is a stressful time to be shopping for stair gates.

The other reason to start early is simply energy. Baby proofing takes an afternoon or two of drilling, sorting, and moving things around, and that is far easier to do while your baby still naps predictably than once they are cruising along the furniture. Some parents find it helpful to start right around the time their baby begins sitting up independently, since that often signals the run up to crawling.

If your baby is already mobile and you have not started, that is completely fine and very common. Start with the highest risk items rather than trying to do everything, and give yourself permission to do the rest over a few weeks. Progress here counts for a lot more than perfection.

The Four Fixes That Matter Most

If you only have one afternoon, spend it on these. They address the hazards that lead to the most serious injuries in young children, and they are the ones safety organizations consistently put at the top of the list.

Anchor furniture and televisions

Dressers, bookcases, shelving units, and freestanding televisions can tip over when a baby pulls up on them or opens a drawer to use as a step. Anti tip straps and brackets are inexpensive and most new furniture includes them in the box. It is worth going around the whole house rather than just the nursery, since the living room television stand and the hallway bookcase are just as reachable to a determined baby.

While you are at it, consider what is sitting on top of furniture. A remote control, a phone, or a bowl of keys on a dresser gives a baby a reason to climb, so moving those items lower removes the temptation entirely.

Lock away medicines, cleaning products, and small batteries

This means everything, not just the obvious bottles under the sink. Vitamins, diaper cream, hand sanitizer, laundry pods, essential oils, and any purse or bag with medication in it all belong somewhere a baby cannot reach. Button batteries deserve special mention because they are found in remotes, greeting cards, thermometers, and small toys, and swallowing one is a medical emergency. Store spares in a locked place and check that battery compartments on household items are screwed shut.

Many parents find it easier to relocate these items to a high shelf than to install latches on every low cabinet. Height is a simpler and more reliable barrier than any latch, especially in the early stages.

Gate the stairs

If you have stairs, gates at both the top and the bottom are the general guidance. The gate at the top should be hardware mounted, meaning it screws into the wall or banister, because pressure mounted gates rely on tension and can shift under weight. Pressure mounted gates are usually fine at the bottom of stairs or in a doorway where a fall is not the risk.

Deal with window cords and windows

Corded blinds are a strangulation hazard, and the safest fix is replacing them with cordless versions in any room your baby spends time in. If replacement is not practical right now, cord cleats mounted high on the wall keep cords wound up and out of reach. Separately, window screens are designed to keep bugs out and will not hold a child's weight, so window guards or stops that limit how far a window opens are worth considering for upper floors.

Room by Room Checklist

Living room

This is where most babies spend their floor time, so it usually gets the most attention. Look at the coffee table and any hearth or fireplace surround for sharp corners at head height, and consider soft guards or simply moving the table out during the crawling months. Television and lamp cords are a favorite target, so cord covers or routing them behind furniture helps. Check that floor lamps are stable, that houseplants are out of reach, and that any decorative bowls holding coins or small objects are moved up.

Fireplaces deserve a second look even when they are not in use, since the hearth is often stone and sits at exactly the height a pulling up baby will meet it. A gate around the fireplace area is a common solution for families who use theirs regularly.

Kitchen

The kitchen has more hazards per square foot than anywhere else, which is why some families simply gate it off during cooking. If that is not practical, focus on the under sink cabinet, the knife drawer, and the oven. Stove knob covers stop a curious toddler from turning on a burner, and turning pot handles inward is a small habit that prevents a lot. Trash cans are worth a thought too, since they tend to hold exactly the things you would not want a baby handling.

Many parents find it helps to designate one low cabinet as the baby cabinet, filled with plastic containers and wooden spoons. Having somewhere they are allowed to open takes a surprising amount of pressure off all the places they are not.

Bathroom

Water is the main concern here. Even a small amount in a tub or bucket is a drowning risk for a baby, so emptying containers right away matters more than any gadget. Keep the bathroom door closed when it is not in use, consider a toilet lock, and check your water heater setting, since many pediatricians suggest keeping it at or below 120 degrees Fahrenheit to reduce scald risk. Medicine cabinets and grooming items like razors and nail scissors should be well above reach.

Nursery and bedrooms

The crib itself should be free of pillows, bumpers, and loose blankets, which lines up with the broader safe sleep guidelines most pediatricians follow. Once your baby can pull to standing, lower the crib mattress to its lowest setting and remove any mobile or hanging toy they could reach. Check that the crib is not next to a window with cords or curtains, and that the changing table is anchored if it is a dresser style piece.

What Changes as Your Baby Grows

Baby proofing is not a one time project, which is the part nobody warns you about. Each new skill unlocks a new set of things to look at, so it helps to revisit your home every couple of months rather than assuming you are done.

Around the time babies start crawling, floor level hazards become the focus: cords, small objects, pet bowls, and doorstops with removable rubber tips. When pulling up begins, the danger zone rises to about waist height, which is when furniture anchoring and coffee table corners suddenly matter. Once your baby is walking and climbing, you are looking at counters, chairs used as ladders, and door handles.

Some babies may skip stages or move through them very quickly, and every baby is different in what they find interesting. The child who ignores the outlet entirely might be fascinated by the toilet, so the most useful approach is watching your own baby and responding to what they are actually drawn to.

The Floor Test

The single most useful thing you can do costs nothing. Get down on your hands and knees in each room and crawl around at your baby's eye level, slowly, looking at everything the way they would. It feels a little silly and it works better than any checklist, because you will spot things specific to your home that no article could predict.

You will notice the gap behind the couch where a phone charger dangles, the loose bit of baseboard, the dog's water bowl, the wobbly plant stand, the tiny screw under the radiator. Parents who do this almost always come away with a handful of items they never would have thought of, and the list tends to be much shorter and more actionable than a generic checklist of two hundred things.

It is worth repeating the floor test every couple of months, and especially after any change to your home such as new furniture, a holiday decoration, or a move. Small objects that could be a choking hazard are the most common find, and a good rule of thumb is that anything that fits through a toilet paper tube is too small to leave within reach.

Where Parents Tend to Overdo It

It is easy to spend a lot of money on baby proofing products that solve problems you do not have. Plug in outlet caps, for example, are widely sold but the small ones can become a choking hazard themselves if a baby pries one out, so sliding outlet covers or full plate replacements are often the better choice. Similarly, corner guards on every surface in the house can be more trouble than they are worth, since some falls and bumps are simply part of learning to move.

The other thing worth saying is that baby proofing does not replace supervision, and no amount of equipment will. Most parents land somewhere in the middle: a home where the serious risks are handled so that the ordinary five second gaps of daily life are not frightening, combined with staying reasonably close while your baby explores. That balance lets you relax a little, which is much of the point.

If you want a second opinion on your home, some pediatric offices have safety handouts specific to your area, and your pediatrician is used to this question at the six and nine month visits. Bringing it up at a checkup is an easy way to get advice tailored to your baby and your setup.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should you start baby proofing your house?
Many parents find that starting around four to six months works well, because it gives you time to make changes before your baby is rolling and crawling with purpose. Babies often become mobile faster than expected, so having the big items handled early means you are not scrambling. Every baby is different, and your pediatrician can help you think through timing based on how your baby is moving.
What should you baby proof first?
The general guidance is to start with the hazards that cause the most serious injuries: anchoring furniture and televisions to the wall, installing gates at the top and bottom of stairs, locking away medicines and cleaning products, and moving window blind cords out of reach. These take the most effort but they address the biggest risks, so many parents tackle them before smaller fixes like outlet covers.
Do you really need baby gates?
If your home has stairs, most pediatricians recommend gates at both the top and the bottom. Gates at the top should be hardware mounted, meaning they screw into the wall, because pressure mounted gates can shift. Gates can also be useful for closing off a kitchen or a room you cannot easily make safe. Some families use a gated play space instead, which works well for many babies.
How do you baby proof furniture that keeps tipping?
Dressers, bookcases, and televisions should be secured to the wall with anti tip straps or brackets. Many pieces of furniture now ship with these included. Some parents also move heavy items to lower drawers so the piece is bottom weighted, and avoid putting toys or remotes on top of furniture, since those give a climbing baby a reason to pull up on it.
Is baby proofing necessary if you always supervise your baby?
Supervision matters enormously, but no parent can watch a baby every second, and most incidents happen in the ordinary gaps: answering the door, using the bathroom, or turning to stir a pot. Baby proofing is best thought of as a backup layer rather than a replacement for supervision. The two work together.
How long do you need to keep baby proofing in place?
Most families keep the major measures such as furniture anchors, stair gates, and locked medicine storage well into the toddler years. Smaller items like cabinet latches often come off once a child understands and follows rules reliably, which varies a lot from child to child. Furniture anchors are usually worth keeping permanently since climbing tends to get more adventurous, not less. If you are unsure about anything specific to your child, talk to your pediatrician.
